Power

How much land would it take to replace petroleum?

Every power source needs land: wells and refineries, solar farms, wind farms, reactors, reservoirs. Pick how much energy to replace, check the assumptions, and see what share of the United States each source would cover. Every number is sourced and every number can be changed.

1 What are we replacing?

Replace it one-for-one, or credit electric efficiency?

The question assumes no decrease in energy use, so by default every joule of petroleum is replaced by one joule from the new source. That is the cautious, upper-end answer.

Most petroleum is burned in engines, which turn most of it into waste heat. Lawrence Livermore estimates U.S. transportation is about 21% efficient, while an electric motor and battery are roughly 3–4 times better. So the same driving could need much less electricity than the petroleum it replaces. Move the slider to see how much that matters.

2 How much power does each source make per square metre?

This is power density: the average watts a plant really delivers over a year (capacity factor included), divided by the land it uses. Higher means less land.

5 Work it out from a real plant

Look up a real solar farm, wind farm or power plant: its nameplate capacity, its land area, and its capacity factor (the share of the year's maximum output it really produces). Then:

power density = capacity × capacity factor ÷ land area

The example (100 MW on 3,400 ha at 35%) is a typical U.S. wind farm: NREL's survey found about 34 ha of total project area per MW (Denholm et al. 2009).

Energy per cubic metre (Layton 2008)

Layton compares sources by the energy held in one cubic metre of space. Fuels are a store of energy packed into a small volume; sunlight and wind are a flow that passes through. That is why oil is about 1016 times denser than sunlight by this measure, and why flows need large collecting areas instead.

Log scale: each gridline is 1,000× the one before. Values from Layton (2008), Table 1 and text. Layton also notes that covering an area about the size of Texas with 10%-efficient panels would match the whole world's 2008 energy use.

What this calculator leaves out

  • Storage and backup. Solar and wind vary by hour and season. Batteries, pumped hydro or backup plants add cost and some land.
  • Transmission lines to move power from where it is made to where it is used.
  • Mining and manufacturing land for materials, except where the source study included it (nuclear figures include uranium mining).
  • Resource limits. Hydro and geothermal are dense but only work in certain places; most good U.S. dam sites are already built.
  • Shared land. Land inside a wind farm is mostly still farmed. Rooftop solar uses no new land at all.
  • Turning electricity into fuel. Planes, ships and some industry use petroleum in ways that are hard to electrify directly.
